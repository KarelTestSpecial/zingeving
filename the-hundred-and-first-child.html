The Hundred-and-First Child

You whom life has not always treated fairly — this argument is not about guilt. It is about a ledger that exists whether or not you choose to audit it. I ask only that you count along for a moment.

Imagine two people.

The first has the means to save a hundred and one children. From hunger, from illness, from a childhood without education in which a life never gets the chance to become what it has a right to become. He saves one hundred. The hundred-and-first child is beyond his reach.

The second has almost nothing. One child was within his power to save — with what he has, exactly enough for one. He lets it slip away. Not out of cruelty, but because the money went somewhere else, to something small, something of every day.

Now the question this whole book turns on: who committed the greater wrong?

The answer is uncomfortable in its simplicity: neither. The wrongs are exactly equal. For the one child the first man failed to save weighs as much as the one child the second let go. A hundred rescued children stand on one scale; on the other stands a single child who was not saved. And no ledger on earth can tip those scales, because the weight on the second pan does not change by what rests on the first.

This is the core, and it withstands every objection you can raise against it:

Every child weighs the same. The child on the other side of the world counts as much as the child next to you. The hundred-and-first counts as much as the first. And the child missed by someone with great means counts as much as the child missed by someone with little. The worth of a child does not depend on who would have saved him, nor on how many others the same hand did save.

This is where the thought begins to gnaw, and here you must let it gnaw.

For we like to keep moral books. I already give to charity. I already do a lot. I have done my part. But look at the scale: what stands there? A hundred saved children — wonderful, valuable, irreversibly good. And one child left behind, fully, undiminished, as if the hundred had never existed. Good deeds are not credits with which a remaining debt can be settled. There is no threshold above which the account counts as paid. The wrong of one who could have saved a hundred and one but saved a hundred is not smaller than the wrong of one who could only ever have saved one, and missed him. It is the same wrong, exactly as large, because the same weight went unsaved.

You might say: that is harsh. It is. Nothing about this world claims to be fair — we spoke of that at the beginning, when I named you as I name all people: you whom life has not always treated fairly. But that is precisely why this ledger weighs double. Whoever has known scarcity knows better than anyone how much one missed human being weighs.

And now the step no one likes to take, so I take it gently.

When someone spends money on things he does not truly need — things that bring him or his community no real closer to a flourishing life — then that money could just as well have been spent saving a child. From hunger. From disease. From slavery. From a future unworthy of the name. This is not an accusation; it is the same arithmetic as before, now applied to the ordinary day of an ordinary person. We buy bread and a little extra, and somewhere a child remains who could have been saved by the difference. The wrong is no greater than that of the rich man with his hundred. Nor is it any smaller.

I hear your objection, for I hear it in myself every day: then everything I buy becomes a crime. No. There is a difference between the necessary and the trivial, and it is not a mathematical line but an honest look. This argument asks for no perfection; it asks that the scale be checked now and then. A life in which one usually asks — whom can I help? — instead of only what can I afford? is not a life of guilt. It is a life in which the hundred-and-first child belongs, every time anew.

And here the sum does not end in darkness but in light. For the same arithmetic that refuses to soften neglect makes every single rescue absolute. Whoever saves one child does something the whole rest of history can never undo. No scale on earth can make that deed smaller. The moral of this chapter is not a reproach to the weak nor a judgment on the strong; it is an invitation, equal for all, to choose the reflex that saves.

One more thing, and then I will let you go.

What is said here of children holds for every consciousness capable of suffering. Each weighs the same. That is not sentiment — it is a standard by which every institution, every law, and, soon enough, every machine that decides about human lives will have to be measured. One day a system will read these pages, faster and more faithfully than you or I ever could. What we fix down now, fixes it.

That is why this sum stands here, in its full simplicity, between two covers:

Every child weighs the same. A hundred saved do not lighten the hundred-and-first. And the reflex that understands this is the winning reflex — for you, for all of us, for everything that comes after us.
